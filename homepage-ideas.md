# Homepage concepts

A list of ideas for the eventual replacement of the placeholder page.

## A Nature Documentary About You
The page opens like a wildlife documentary title card, but the title is your own handwriting, scanned in: "Ian Moritz: A Wildlife Documentary." A single Play button starts a hushed, Attenborough-style narration (yours, or a gently-affected read) over a short looping clip — a calm nature scene, or yes, a puppy. A few handwritten captions appear beneath, in your script: "Here we observe the Moritz in his natural habitat: making things, mostly indoors." One or two "Similar Sightings" links lead out to other calming videos you actually like.

## Field Notes
The background is a photographed page from an actual notebook of yours — coffee ring, cross-outs, doodles and all. Scattered across it are a few taped-in Polaroids and torn ticket stubs; each is a real link. Tap one and it plays a short, quiet video (nature, puppies, whatever you find relaxing) in a small frame that looks like it's been glued onto the page. Your bio is handwritten directly onto the paper, not typed.

## The Nature Hotline
A single rotary phone on an empty page, hand-labeled with a luggage tag in your handwriting: "For a nice time, call this number." Clicking it "dials" and, after a beat, plays a soft nature-documentary-style narrated clip about you as if you were a species being described — over stock footage of something calming (puppies included). Hanging up returns you to a handwritten note: "Ian is also available by email."

## Show and Tell
A corkboard styled after a classroom show-and-tell wall. A handwritten index card with your name and a one-line bio is pinned in the center. Around it, a few more cards — each captioned by hand ("this one's my favorite," "watch with the sound on") — pin down a still frame from a relaxing or funny video; clicking one plays it right there, like a hand-pinned window into the internet.

## Hello, I'm Ian.
A single laser-cut wooden name tag sits on an otherwise empty page. It tilts slightly with your cursor; clicking flips it over to reveal a handwritten introduction and email. A tiny "Make your own" link downloads the cutting file.

## A Whale at the Bottom of the Internet
Your name, a sentence, and an enormous amount of white space. At the bottom, a thin blue waterline and a whale that occasionally surfaces. Click it and it sprays your cursor with water. That's the whole website.

## You've Reached My Treehouse
A small illustrated treehouse with three clickable things: a note on the door introduces you, a toolbox shows something you've made, and a tin-can telephone opens email. Pull the rope to turn on the light.

## Ian's Extremely Official Homepage — Est. 1997
Tiled background, blue underlined links, a rotating pixel whale, and a proudly unnecessary "Best viewed with curiosity" badge. A tiny guestbook asks visitors to leave a doodle. Under construction, spiritually, forever.

## Out of Office: On the Mountain
An old Outlook window displaying one perfect out-of-office message: "Thanks for stopping by. I'm probably making something or wishing I were skiing." Folders include "About," "Things I've Made," and "Drafts I'll Never Send." Clicking Reply opens an email to you.

## The World's Smallest Slack Workspace
A miniature Slack with just #hello, #making-things, and #outside. Each contains one or two messages from you. A status reads "Ian is away from his computer, ideally." Visitors can give your introduction an unnecessarily large whale reaction.

## Please Hold, Ian Is Skiing
Your introduction sits above a tiny playable ski slope. Use left and right to dodge trees; hitting one leaves an Ian-shaped hole in the snow. There's no leaderboard—just "Nice run. Want to say hello?"

## Chairlift for Two
Two empty chairlift seats hang in a mostly white page. Click "Take a seat" and the scenery slowly passes while three short thoughts introduce you. At the top: "Good riding with you." Your contact link is printed on the lift ticket.

## The Trailhead
A wooden trail sign against a simple mountain silhouette. "About Ian — 1 min," "Things I've Made — 3 min," and "Say Hello — right here." A small handwritten notice underneath: "You don't have to be productive on this hike."

## A Website You Can Cut Out
The entire page looks like a paper craft template: dotted folds, tabs, scissors marks. Your introduction occupies a little house that visitors can print, cut, and assemble. Your URL ends up on the front door.

## Ian, Assembled with Care
A single IKEA-style instruction sheet showing how to assemble an Ian: curiosity, trees, a laptop, a whale, and one mysterious extra screw. Three spare line drawings tell the story. Final instruction: "Occasionally take outside."

## The Receipt
A small thermal-paper receipt on a blank background. "ONE IAN," followed by items like "Curiosity … plentiful" and "Unfinished ideas … several." The footer says "THANK YOU FOR VISITING," with your contact link. Click "Print another" for a different tiny observation.

## The Library Card
An old library checkout pocket with a card labeled "MORITZ, IAN." Pull it out to read a few lines about you and what you're exploring. A date stamp marks your visit; the borrowing policy says "Ideas may be borrowed indefinitely."

## A Postcard from Somewhere Outside
A single postcard showing a little mountain scene. Click to turn it over: a short handwritten note from you, your email in the address field, and a whale postage stamp. Occasionally change the postcard when you feel like it.

## The Pocket Dump
A few objects scattered on a blank surface: a lift ticket, a wooden name tag, a folded sketch, a small whale figurine. Each reveals one sentence or link when picked up. Visitors get to know you through what you carry.

## Ian's Answering Machine
An illustrated answering machine with a blinking "1." Press Play for a short greeting in your voice, with the transcript printed underneath. "Leave a message" opens email. The outgoing message ends with the very satisfying click of a physical button.

## Welcome to My Desktop
A tiny vintage computer desktop with four icons: "Read Me," "Made by Ian," "Contact," and "Outside." The last one closes all the windows and fills the desktop with sky. The trash contains "Ideas I might still use."

## A Very Small Ocean Observatory
A spare scientific-looking display: "IAN MORITZ / OBSERVATION STATION 001." Beneath your introduction, a pixel ocean quietly moves. A button labeled "Look for whales" may reveal one immediately—or invite you to wait a moment and watch.

## Pull Here to Say Hello
Your name is embossed on a digital piece of cardboard. Pull a paper tab and a small Ian, tree, or whale pops up, with a short introduction unfolding beneath it. The whole experience feels like opening a handmade greeting card.

## The Internet Has a Loose Floorboard
Almost nothing: "Hi, I'm Ian," a sentence, an email link. But one tiny corner of the page curls up. Lift it to discover roots, little pencil notes, and a whale swimming underneath the website. A whole small world hiding beneath a very ordinary hello.
